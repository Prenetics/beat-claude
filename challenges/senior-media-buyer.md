## The Challenge

Picture this: you've just been handed the keys to a large DTC Meta account — eight figures a year, thousands of live ads, real money moving every hour. Somewhere in this account, things are going wrong. ROAS is sliding, CPM is climbing at flat CTR, and nobody can tell you exactly why.

This is a fictional scenario, but it's built on the kind of problems that break real accounts at scale. Your job over the next three hours: show us you would (a) build a resilient account, (b) run it under explicit rules rather than vibes, (c) spot problems before they compound into a bad month, and (d) turn creative insight into ads and editor briefs that can be tested at scale.

**Scenario assumptions *(all illustrative)*:**

- Account scale: roughly **$300K/day** in Meta spend across thousands of live ads.
- Target: blended new-customer CAC **under $200**, measured in Shopify (source of truth), full completed days only. Assume Meta attribution runs ~15% under Shopify.
- Three main prospecting "lanes" (distinct audience/angle groupings). Historical testing suggests each has a daily spend ceiling beyond which CPA degrades sharply: **Lane A ~$100K/day, Lane B ~$25K/day, Lane C ~$8K/day.**
- Creative supply: ~40–60 genuinely new concepts per month, plus iterations.

Keep answers concise. Tables, annotated screenshots, rough layouts and scripts are welcome. Production polish is not scored.

## Part 1 — Account build (30 points)

Design the account architecture you would run at this scale. Be specific enough that a junior buyer could implement it from your answer.

Cover, at minimum:

1. **Campaign structure.** How many campaigns, organised by what (lane, funnel stage, geo, objective)? Where do you use CBO vs ABO, and why? Where does testing live vs scaling?
2. **Ad set build.** How many ads per ad set, and what determines that number? What is your position on large shared ad sets (25–40 ads) given Meta gives you no cap, floor, or weighting on delivery within a set?
3. **Budget allocation and floors.** How do you guarantee a new concept actually gets spend rather than being starved by an incumbent winner? What minimum daily spend does a concept need before its results mean anything, and why?
4. **Bidding.** When do you run cost caps, when lowest-cost, when tROAS/value optimization? What is the failure mode of value optimization at low signal volume, and how would you detect it within 48 hours?
5. **Hygiene.** Naming conventions, exclusion audiences (what goes on prospecting, what must never), change logging, and how account state stays legible to someone who didn't make the changes. Show how your naming connects each ad to its concept, angle, hook, format and version so creative learning survives beyond an individual ad ID.

## Part 2 — Rules of engagement (20 points)

Write your standing rules for this account as if they were going into an SOP. Each rule needs a threshold, a check cadence, and the action it triggers. Cover at least:

- **Kill rules.** At what ROAS/CPA and after how much spend does an ad die? A flat "<0.5 ROAS = kill" is on the table — defend it, refine it, or replace it. What spend floor must an ad clear before any read is valid?
- **Pause vs delete.** Which do you use and why? What happens to learning-phase progress in each case, and what does that imply for how you handle seasonal winners you'll want back?
- **Scaling rules.** What is your maximum daily budget increase on a performing ad set, and what happens to learning phase if you exceed it? (Our house ceiling is +18%/day — tell us whether you'd keep it and how you'd scale a winner from $5K/day to $40K/day under it.)
- **The pairing rule.** May a campaign receive a budget increase in the same week ads inside it are paused? Explain the mechanism behind your answer.
- **Cost caps.** Where you use them, how you set the cap relative to target CPA, and how you respond when a cost-capped campaign stops spending.

## Part 4 — Creative judgment and production (20 points)

You have access to editors and designers. Your job is to decide what they should make, explain why it should work, and make the brief clear enough to execute without another strategy meeting.

Use IM8 Daily Ultimate Essentials for your original concepts. Choose one market and keep the product, market and offer consistent across the two concepts so we can assess the difference in creative thinking. Use current public IM8 materials for facts and cite the source of any product, pricing or offer claim. If a needed asset or claim cannot be verified, label the dependency rather than inventing it.

### A. Identify strong creative (4 points)

Select two real ads from IM8 or relevant DTC brands: one you would back for testing, and one that looks compelling but you would not back in its current form. Include a screenshot or link and the date accessed.

For each, explain:

- Who it is for, the buying motivation or objection it addresses, and the core angle.
- How the opening hook, visual, proof, offer and CTA work together.
- What expectation the ad creates before the click, and what the landing page must deliver.
- What you would keep, change or reject — and why.

Separate your hypothesis from known performance. An ad being live, looking polished or attracting engagement does not establish that it acquires customers profitably.

### B. Create two original IM8 ad concepts (6 points)

Develop one static ad and one 20–30 second video ad. They must address meaningfully different motivations or objections. Changing a colour, headline or creator alone does not count as a new concept.

For each concept, provide:

- **Audience and insight:** who it is for, what they want, and what stops them buying.
- **Angle and hypothesis:** the central message and why you expect it to improve purchase behaviour.
- **Actual ad copy:** primary text and headline, three hook options and two CTA options. Write the words you would run; "strong hook" or "shop now CTA" is not a brief.
- **Visual execution:** what the viewer sees and how the product, proof and offer appear.
- **Click destination:** the page you would use and the message it must reinforce.

Include a rough static mockup showing the actual headline, visual arrangement and CTA. A slide, Canva/Figma layout or AI-assisted mockup is sufficient. The video's script and storyboard belong in the editor brief below.

### C. Turn the video concept into an editor-ready brief (6 points)

Write a production brief an editor could execute immediately. Include:

1. **Objective and message hierarchy:** what the viewer must understand, believe and do.
2. **A time-coded script/storyboard:** opening frame, exact voiceover or dialogue, on-screen text, visual direction, proof, product demonstration and final CTA.
3. **Asset requirements:** footage, product imagery, talent, screenshots or evidence needed. Identify what can be reused and what needs to be sourced or shot.
4. **Six controlled variations:** use your three hooks and two CTAs to specify six versions of the same core video. Label each version, what changes and what stays fixed. These are iterations of one concept, not six new concepts.
5. **Export and handoff instructions:** 1080×1920 and 1080×1080 versions of each variation, framing and text placement for both formats, file naming that maps to your account taxonomy, and a short quality-control checklist.

Use sachet, not scoop. Do not invent testimonials, results, product benefits or offers. Do not use "clinically proven"; if you use an Essentials trial claim, include "in a 12-week randomized controlled trial" and source the exact claim. Flag any permission or asset dependency.

You are writing the brief, not producing six finished videos. We score the clarity of your creative decisions and whether an editor could execute them.

### D. Connect production to a buying decision (4 points)

Give us a concise test-and-learning plan:

- Which concept launches first, where it sits in your Part 1 structure, and how it gets a fair spend allocation.
- Whether the six video variations launch together or in stages, and why that fits the available budget and signal.
- The purchase/CAC evidence that determines scale, iterate or stop, consistent with Part 2. Explain how you use hook, hold and click metrics to diagnose performance without treating them as proof of profitable acquisition.
- What you would change if an ad wins attention but not purchases; how you would distinguish fatigue from poor delivery or a weak offer/page.
- One example of a performance finding translated into a specific instruction in the next editor brief.

Finally, show in a few lines how you would organise the monthly supply of 40–60 new concepts plus iterations: prioritisation, briefing, production, launch and feedback. Explain how you prevent a high volume of cosmetic variations from masquerading as creative diversity.

## What We're Looking For

- You understand that at this scale, **allocation and structure can fail before demand does** — efficiency can be recovered by subtraction, not just more budget.
- You scale by **rules, thresholds, and logs** — not by feel.
- You can tell a **creative failure from a configuration failure**, and recognise when a landing page or offer is the constraint.
- You know what the platform's mechanics (learning phase, delivery concentration, attribution lag) actually do, versus folklore.
- You can identify promising creative and explain the mechanism: audience insight, hook, visual, proof, objection, offer and CTA.
- You can write actual ads and actionable editor briefs, then turn performance evidence into the next production decision.
- You distinguish a new concept from an iteration, and organise production to learn rather than merely increase asset count.
- A colleague could **reconstruct your decisions from your logs** a week later.

---

## What We're NOT Looking For

- Re-inflating budgets to hit a volume target without evidence of additional profitable capacity or a credible creative plan.
- Trusting Meta-attributed ROAS as the decision number with no Shopify cross-check.
- Deleting ads as routine hygiene.
- Killing or scaling anything on intraday data.
- Plans with no change-logging anywhere — decisions nobody else can trace.
- Calling an ad a winner because its CTR, views or engagement are high.
- "Make more UGC", "test better hooks" or other directions an editor cannot execute.
- Cosmetic variations presented as genuinely new concepts.
- Invented claims, unsupported testimonials or offers the business has not approved.
- A beautiful creative presentation that avoids the account-management and commercial decisions.
